## Philosophy

As technology becomes more capable, engineers do not need less understanding. They need a different
kind. Students must know not only how a method works, but **when to use it, what it assumes, where it
can fail, and what happens when its output reaches a physical system.**

The moments I enjoy most as an educator are when an idea that first seems abstract becomes something
a student can implement, test, question, and use on their own. I have found that students build that
confidence in three stages:

<div class="pillars">
  <div><span class="card-num">01</span><h3>Why it matters</h3><p>Understand the engineering problem and its stakes.</p></div>
  <div><span class="card-num">02</span><h3>How to approach it</h3><p>Learn the principles and tools needed to address it.</p></div>
  <div><span class="card-num">03</span><h3>See it work</h3><p>Apply those tools to a problem whose behavior they can observe.</p></div>
</div>

In manufacturing, physical systems complete this progression especially well. A student learning
optimization should see how a scheduling decision changes the operation of a real system. A student
learning machine learning should see what happens when sensor data are noisy or conditions drift from
the training data. A student learning robotics should face the challenge of coordinating equipment,
not just programming an isolated robot.

## Mentoring

As a senior member of my current research laboratory, I work closely with graduate students on their
research. My goal as a mentor is to help students learn to **identify worthwhile problems themselves**:
to observe a limitation in a physical system, formulate a research question, choose or develop a
method, evaluate it critically, and recognize the new questions their results open up.
